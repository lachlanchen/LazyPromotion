# When Rules Stop Feeling Shared, I Return to the Chinese Classics

Lately, the news has kept bringing me back to a question: what happens to a relationship built on promises when the people involved no longer agree on what those promises mean?

Alliances shift. Agreements are reinterpreted. An action described as an exception can become the argument for the next exception. The names change every day, but some of the questions feel much older.

That is one reason I have been returning to the *Zuo Tradition*, or *Zuozhuan*, and one reason I built Bunko, a reader for Chinese, Japanese and English classics.

## A river crossing, and a decision to wait

An account in the *Zuozhuan*, under the twenty-second year of Duke Xi, describes a battle between Song and Chu at Hong. Song's troops were ready while Chu's army was still crossing the river. An officer proposed attacking. Duke Xiang of Song refused. When Chu had crossed but had not yet formed its ranks, he declined another opportunity. Song eventually lost, and the duke was wounded.

Afterward, the duke defended his restraint. Ziyu challenged it: once they were fighting an enemy, why give up an opportunity to win? The account and their disagreement are available in the [original Chinese text](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/春秋左氏傳/僖公#僖公二十二年).

It is easy to turn this into a story about a foolish commander. I find a different question more interesting. Was he observing a shared constraint, or a personal standard whose cost he alone was willing to bear? What happens when an opponent does not accept the same limits? And what happens if everyone abandons restraint because they suspect everyone else will?

The passage does not establish that all warfare in early China followed one consistent code of honor. It records disagreement. Flattening the period into “first there were rules, then there was force” loses much of what makes the text worth reading.

## Read the argument, not just the lesson

The modern world is not the world of these states. Its institutions, laws, weapons and consequences are different. Replacing an ancient ruler's name with a name from today's headlines would not make a useful historical explanation.

What the classics offer is a way to ask more precise questions. In the *Zuozhuan*, I look at who makes a promise and who bears the cost of keeping it. In the *Strategies of the Warring States*, I ask why an alliance makes sense at one moment and stops making sense at another. Reading *Han Feizi*, I think about why institutions cannot rely entirely on a ruler's good intentions.

These are not ready-made answers. They preserve arguments, choices and the consequences the texts describe. That matters to readers outside China too. You do not need to recognize every state's name to begin noticing who is afraid, who is buying time, and who pays for somebody else's decision.
